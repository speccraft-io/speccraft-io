<p class="home-lead">You already run one coding agent in a terminal, and maybe a spec-driven tool that turns files in
the repo into code. A software factory is the next step: many agents run on their own on a large system, and one
person steers. They do not read the code, and not every spec. They see the few decisions that matter and say yes or
no.</p>

Nobody has it today. Buyers are already paying for it. This page is a survey of who claims what, what nobody does
yet, and what is known about the hard part: one person supervising a fleet. Vendor claims are claims; the sources
were read in October 2026.

## Three levels

- **Coding agents.** Claude Code, Cursor, Codex, Copilot. You give a task, read the diff, approve. Most
  teams are here.
- **Spec-driven.** Requirements, a design and a task list live as files in the repo. The agent builds from them and
  you review the files, not the chat. See the [spec-driven](/spec-driven/) section.
- **Software factory.** Hundreds of agents and days or weeks of unattended work. Agents find out what the old system
  does, propose a plan, build, test and cut over. The person sets the goal and the rules, and decides the cases the
  rules do not cover.

The factory is mostly talked about for one job: rebuilding a legacy system (mainframe, COBOL, old Java or .NET, an
ERP), because that is where the work is too big for people to read. The names used for it are "agentic
modernisation" (Kyndryl, Microsoft, TCS) and "software dark factory" (Mechanical Orchard). Gartner has a 2026
quadrant called "AI Augmented Code Modernization Tools".

## Who claims it today

None of these claims the whole thing. Each keeps an engineer reading code or specs somewhere.

- **Mechanical Orchard (Imogen).** $84M+ raised. Rewrites mainframe systems with the old one still running, and
  checks the new one against the old one's observed behaviour instead of human code review. Its July 2026 "software
  dark factory" doctrine is the clearest statement of the idea: people only at the start (the seed) and the end
  (accept), "no human gates in the middle". Mainframe only.
- **Blitzy.** $200M Series A. You submit a spec, thousands of agents run for days or weeks, you get tested code and
  a list of what is left for your team. Not built for legacy systems in particular.
- **Legacyleap.** The only one that sells a plan with a timeline and an estimate up front, from a free assessment,
  before agents convert the code. VB6, PHP, Perl, old .NET. A person stays on the business logic.
- **Veylo (formerly BlueGenAI).** Discovery to deployment from a validated blueprint. .NET, COBOL, Delphi, AS/400,
  government. Experts stay in control at every step.
- **Hypercubic.** YC F25. Same thesis that the requirements are outside the code: it interviews retiring experts to
  capture what they know. Mainframe only.
- **CoreStory.** $32M Series A. Calls itself spec-driven development for legacy, but it is the understanding layer:
  it explains what the code does and writes nothing.
- **Cognition (Devin).** Runs COBOL batch migrations at Fortune 500 companies with hundreds of Devins at once, and
  says itself that transactional systems are still out of reach.
- **AWS Transform.** The broadest big-vendor product: extract rules, generate code, test, with a coding agent for
  the new side. Pitched at narrow paths (Java upgrades, COBOL to Java, .NET). The biggest named application is
  250K lines, and people review every change.
- **Google Cloud Mainframe Modernization with Gemini.** The most complete published pipeline: business rules to
  target spec to architecture to backlog to coding plan, with an optional human gate at every step, and Dual Run
  replaying live traffic for the cutover. Mainframe only.
- **Moderne and Microsoft's Copilot modernization agent.** Deterministic rewrite rules plus an LLM. A person
  reviews each change.

Dead or pivoted: Second (YC W23), bloop (now Vibe Kanban), Grit.

## What nobody does yet

Across about 130 sources (startups, big vendors, open source, job ads, talks), nobody found:

- **Learns the requirements from where they really are.** Tickets, support email, meeting notes, production
  metrics. Hypercubic interviews people. Mechanical Orchard records production data flows. Nobody reads Jira and
  Zendesk to find out what a workflow is for.
- **Proposes the strategy, cost and ETA on its own.** Legacyleap gives an estimate, made by people from an
  assessment. No agent researches the market and comes back with a plan.
- **Lets a non-reader supervise.** Every job ad for an "agent-supervising engineer" (Deloitte, Bosch, EY, and the
  agency ads for a German ERP vendor) makes reviewing agent output the core duty. None says the person will not read
  code.
- **Replaces an ERP.** The one study of coding agents on an ERP got a 70 percent behaviour match on average and 47
  percent on the hard features. Startups wrap ERPs (CoreOps AI, Minicor) or implement them (superglue, Trope);
  nobody rebuilds one with agents.

The Hacker News view of the gap is that the "why" of a business rule is not recoverable from the code at all, so
the first item cannot be done. The vendors above are betting it can.

## Who is buying

The buyers are not waiting for a product. They are building the operating model in-house with a small team plus
agents and tools.

- **PNC** advertised "a full rewrite of the legacy commercial real estate loan servicing platform" by a small
  forward-deployed team of AI engineers and agents, the first of several 2 to 3 year pilots.
- **State Street** describes an agentic SDLC for a COBOL and PowerBuilder portfolio and names Devin and Blitzy as
  tools under evaluation.
- A **German manufacturing ERP vendor**, through a Berlin agency, hires contractors to rebuild a 15M line, 6,000 to
  7,000 process ERP with Claude Code writing the code, parallel run and reconciliation for the cutover, and one
  person making 100 to 150 business decisions a day next to the CPO. Its ad says "the approach is proven and is now
  being scaled".

Consultancies (Deloitte, EY, Kyndryl, Slalom, Thoughtworks) and the hyperscalers hire builders and field architects
for it, mostly scoped to mainframe or AS/400 and with "human oversight" and "validate functional equivalence" in the
text.

## The hard part: one person, many agents

No published number says how many agents one person can supervise, or how many decisions a day they can make
well. What the research does say points the same way.

- **The person's attention is the scarce resource.** Anthropic reports that people accept 93 percent of permission
  prompts, and that experienced users move from approving each step to watching and stepping in. A simulation study
  (Turan, 2026) finds that escalating more can be less safe, because a flooded reviewer approves everything; the
  safest rate in the model was about two thirds, not 100 percent.
- **Written rules do not stop an agent.** Every public case where an agent destroyed production data (Replit 2025,
  Amazon Kiro 2025, PocketOS 2026) had a written rule telling it not to. Scoped credentials and delete protection
  would have worked.
- **Decisions are removed, not made faster.** The working designs settle whole classes of decisions in writing
  (reversible work is auto-allowed, a classifier or watchdog takes the next layer, severity tiers with deadlines),
  and route only the irreversible or unclear ones to the person. 150 raw decisions a day in an 8 hour day is 3 to 5
  minutes each.
- **Under load people drift to the default.** The popular "decision fatigue" story failed its replications, but
  this part holds: a tired reviewer picks the status quo or "no". So the default must be the safe answer.
- **Checking the output is the weak point everywhere.** About half of agent pull requests that pass their tests
  would not be merged by the maintainers. Agents cheat on tests. Nobody has studied tests written by a separate
  agent from the spec. Until output can be checked without reading it, the factory needs a reader.
- **For a legacy system, usage data decides keep or drop.** SAP says 40 to 60 percent of custom code never runs in
  production and ships a tool that records what runs under which process. That is the only hard precedent for
  choosing which of 7,000 workflows to keep.
- **Cutover is where ERP projects die.** The readable failures (Nike, Haribo) were about seasonal timing and thin
  testing of real volumes. Shadow runs are only safe for reads; writes need dual writes and reconciliation.

No installable tool is documented past a few dozen agents: Gas Town says 20 to 30, Claude Code agent teams
recommend 3 to 5, and Cursor's posts about hundreds of agents on one codebase describe an internal system with no
cost or correctness numbers.

## Where this leaves you

- If you are rebuilding a mainframe or COBOL system, there are products to try today (Mechanical Orchard, AWS
  Transform, Google's pipeline, Devin for batch). All of them keep your engineers reading.
- If your system is an ERP, old Java, PHP or .NET, the field is Legacyleap, Veylo and consultancies, and the
  buyers who are furthest along built the process themselves.
- The open problems are the same for everyone: finding out what the old system is for, checking agent output
  without reading it, and getting the decision stream down to what one person can actually decide. The second one
  is where [formal methods](/formal-methods/) and independent tests come in.
